How Does a Whole Collection Get Built From Nothing?

A collection goes concept, line-up, patterns, samples, run. The concept decides what the collection is about. The line-up decides what is in it and in what order. The patterns decide whether it can be made. The samples decide whether it should be. You arrive with a direction and a deadline, and you leave with a collection that exists.

Not every commission is one garment. I build whole lines too, and the method is the same as a single piece, applied at a different scale and against a harder deadline.

What does the concept stage actually decide?

What the collection is about, and what it refuses to be about.

This is the part that people expect to be the loosest and it is the part I try hardest to close. A direction that can be said in one sentence produces a coherent line. A direction that needs a paragraph produces twelve garments that were each a good idea separately.

We work out the story, the materials, the silhouettes, the price position if there is one, and the constraints. The constraints matter most. A tour has a schedule. A launch has a date. A small brand has a budget that decides how many pieces can be sampled at all.

What is a line-up and why is it done early?

A line-up is every piece in the collection laid out in order, as flats or sketches, seen together.

It is the cheapest edit you will ever get. On a line-up you can see that four pieces do the same job, that there is no way into the collection at the opening, that everything is the same length, or that a whole material is carrying a single garment.

Cutting a piece at line-up costs a drawing. Cutting it at sample costs a pattern, a length of cloth and two weeks. That is the entire argument for doing it properly.

What happens at pattern stage?

The idea meets the fact of a body.

Every piece gets a pattern drawn and a toile cut, exactly as a private commission does. A full version in plain cotton, on a stand or on a fit model, marked and taken apart until the shape is right. The five steps are the same ones I use for a single garment.

For a collection there is an extra question at this stage: can this be made more than once. A shape that only works because of an hour of hand manipulation is a beautiful sample and a problem in a run of forty. This is where my years buying and planning for houses do the most work. Buying teaches you what a factory can and cannot do to a pattern, and it teaches you that quickly.

How many samples does it take?

More than one, always, and the number depends on how new the shapes are.

The industry has a whole vocabulary for this: proto samples for construction, fit samples for sizing, salesman samples for selling, size set samples to prove the grading. Techpacker's breakdown of the sample types is a good map of the full industrial version, and StartUp Fashion's sampling walkthrough explains why nobody gets it right in one round.

Working at my scale, the vocabulary collapses. There is the first sample, which shows whether the shape is real, and the corrected sample, which is the one everything else gets made from. The first piece decides the rest of them.

What decides whether a collection can be made on time?

The number of new patterns, not the number of garments.

Ten pieces built on four patterns is a much smaller job than six pieces on six patterns. That is not a compromise, it is how collections have always been built: a shape is proved once and then varied in length, cloth, sleeve and finish. Repetition across a line-up reads as a point of view rather than as economy, as long as it is deliberate.

So when a deadline is tight, I edit the pattern count first and the garment count second.

What is different about a run compared to a single piece?

Consistency becomes the whole job.

One garment has to be right. Forty have to be identical, which means the cutting has to be controlled, the sewing order fixed, and the finishing standard written down rather than remembered. Cut, made and finished on one table, in the numbers a schedule actually needs.

Small runs made properly, rather than large ones made quickly. That is the trade I offer and it is an honest one: I am not going to be the cheapest way to make four hundred units, and for a first collection or a touring wardrobe, four hundred units is rarely the question anyway.

What do I need to bring to start?

A direction and a deadline. In that order of importance, but the deadline gets asked about first.

Useful to bring: what the collection is for, how many pieces you imagine, whether it needs to be sized or made to individual measurements, the budget shape, and any dates that will not move. A show, a launch, a first show date on a tour.

Not necessary to bring: finished sketches. If you have them, good. If you have a mood and a reason, that is enough to start.

The collections work is described here, and if you already know what you are building, tell me what it is and when it has to exist.

Questions people ask

How is a fashion collection developed from scratch? Concept, then line-up, then patterns and toiles, then samples, then the run. Each stage is an edit, and the earlier a piece is cut from the collection the less it costs.

How long does building a collection take? It depends on the number of new patterns rather than the number of garments. Bring the deadline first, because it decides how many new patterns are possible.

Can you make a small run rather than a large one? Yes. Small runs made properly, in the quantity you need and the time you have, are what this atelier is set up for.

Do you make touring wardrobes as well as collections? Yes. A touring wardrobe is a collection with duplicates and a repair schedule attached, built for quick changes and repeat wear.

Do I need finished designs before I write? No. A direction and a deadline are enough to start. The first conversation is free and nothing is decided in it.

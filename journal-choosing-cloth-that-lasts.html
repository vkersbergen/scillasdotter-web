How Do You Choose Cloth for a Piece You Want to Wear in Ten Years?

I pick fabric for how long it will last, not for how it photographs this season. That means fibre before finish, weight before drape, and testing how a cloth behaves after it has been sat in, packed, cleaned and hung. A garment cut for one person deserves cloth that outlives the mood it was bought in.

Cloth is the largest single decision in a commission after the shape, and it is the one people arrive with the least language for. So here is how I think about it.

Why does cloth choice matter more in a made to measure piece?

Because the piece is not replaceable.

Off the rack, a fabric that goes soft after two seasons is annoying. In a commission it wastes months of work and a pattern that was drawn from your body. The cloth has to be worth the hours going into it, and the hours are most of what a couture piece is.

There is also the plainer argument. WRAP's work on clothing durability has spent years showing that extending the active life of a garment is the single largest lever on its carbon, water and waste footprint. A piece you still wear in ten years is not a moral position. It is just the point of making it this way.

What do you look at first?

The fibre, before anything else.

Silk, wool, linen and cotton behave in ways I can predict over years. They crease and recover in known ways, they take a press, they age rather than degrade, and they can be repaired. Blends can be excellent and they can also be a way of making a cheap cloth feel expensive in the shop and nowhere else.

I am not against synthetics. There are places where they are the right answer, particularly for stage work where recovery under load matters more than anything. For an evening piece meant to last, natural fibres do more of the work.

What is the second thing?

Weight, and specifically whether the weight matches what the shape is asking for.

Every design has a weight it wants. A sculpted collar needs a cloth with body, so silk gazar or a firm mikado does the work and a soft crepe cannot. A bias-cut column needs a cloth that will fall, so a heavy faille fights it. Half the fabrics that disappoint people were simply the wrong weight for the drawing.

This is one reason the toile matters. I can cut a cotton in roughly the right weight and see whether the shape is asking for more body or less before we spend anything.

How do you actually test a cloth?

By abusing a length of it in the atelier, and by looking at it under the light it will be seen in.

Crush a corner in your fist for ten seconds and open your hand. Some cloths come back, some hold the crease for an hour, some hold it forever. That tells you what an evening in a car and a chair will look like.

Hang it over an edge and look at the fold. Pull it on the bias and let go. Hold it up to a window and see what the light does through it, because a lining decision is made there. Look at it under hard side light, which finds every irregularity, and under a warm lamp, which forgives everything.

If it is going on camera or under a stage light, it gets looked at there too, because colour moves under those and a fine repeating weave can shimmer on a lens in a way it never does in a room.

What questions should you ask about a cloth?

Five, and none of them are technical.

How does it clean, and how often will it need to. What does it do when it creases. Will it wear at the seat, the elbow and the underarm, which are the three places a garment tells the truth. Can it be let out or taken in later. And can it be repaired if something happens to it.

That last one matters more than it sounds. A cloth you cannot invisibly mend is a cloth that gets one accident.

Does more expensive mean it lasts longer?

Not reliably.

Price in fabric buys fineness, rarity, hand finishing and a name, and only some of those correlate with lifespan. A very fine silk chiffon is expensive and delicate. A good wool crepe is cheaper and will outlive it by a decade.

What I look for is whether the cloth is well made for what it is: an even weave, a stable hand, a finish that is part of the fabric rather than sitting on top of it. A finish that washes out is a cloth pretending to be a better one.

What about a wedding dress specifically?

A wedding dress has an unusual life. One long day, then decades in a box, then possibly a second life.

So I think about two things people rarely raise. First, what happens on the day: fourteen hours, sitting, a meal, dancing, someone standing on the hem. Second, what happens after: whether the cloth yellows, whether the structure holds its shape in storage, and whether the piece could be altered later if it is going to be worn again by anyone.

Ivory and white cloths age differently by fibre, and that is a conversation worth having before the choice is made rather than after. The bridal work is here, and the timeline that goes with it is set out in the process.

Where does the cloth conversation happen?

In the first conversation, after the occasion and the date and before the drawing is finished.

I would rather show you three cloths that will do what the shape needs than fifty that photograph well. Bring the occasion, the venue and the time of year. Write to me and we can start there, or read how the atelier works first.

Questions people ask

What fabrics last longest in a couture garment? Well-made natural fibres, generally. Wool crepe, good silk faille and firm silk weaves age rather than degrade, take a press, and can be repaired. Fibre and construction matter more than price.

How do I test a fabric before choosing it? Crush a corner and see whether it recovers, hang it over an edge to see the fold, pull it on the bias, and look at it under both hard light and warm light. If it is for camera or stage, look at it under that light too.

Is expensive fabric always better? No. Price buys fineness, rarity and finish, and not all of those extend lifespan. A cheaper wool crepe often outlasts a more expensive chiffon.

Can a made to measure piece be altered later? Often yes, and the cloth decides how much room there is. Your pattern stays in the atelier, which makes a later alteration or a second piece considerably easier.

Do you choose the cloth or do I? Both. You bring the occasion and what you like. I bring what the shape needs and what will survive it, and we choose from the overlap.

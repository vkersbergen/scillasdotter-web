Why Are Stage Clothes Built to a Different Standard Than Clothes for a Room?

Stage clothes are built for quick changes, hot light and the same piece worn eighty nights in a row. That changes the seams, the closures, the cloth and the number of copies. A dress for a dinner has to survive one evening. A dress for a tour has to survive a schedule, a truck and a laundry cycle.

I spent years on the road as a wardrobe tailor, on The Monster Ball with Lady Gaga, The Funhouse Carnival Tour with Pink, Last Girl on Earth with Rihanna, and Michael Jackson The Immortal World Tour with Cirque du Soleil. Those are production credits, so I can name them. What I make for a private client stays between us and always will.

A stage teaches you things a fitting room cannot. Nothing gets a second take.

What does a stage actually do to a garment?

It pulls it, heats it, soaks it and repeats.

Performers move in ways nobody moves in a room. Arms above the head, full turns, floor work, running. A seam that is comfortable at a dinner table is under real load at the end of a chorus. Stage lighting is hot, so a performer sweats through a costume every night, and the cloth has to come back from that. A guide to theatre costuming from the American Association of Community Theatre is blunt about the practical side of it, and the practical side is most of the job.

Then there is the truck. A costume travels packed, and it has to hang out overnight and look like itself again by the afternoon.

How does a quick change change the cutting?

It moves the whole design onto the closures.

A change measured in seconds cannot use twenty buttons. You build for hooks, snap tape, hidden zips, magnets, sometimes a breakaway seam, and everything is placed where a dresser standing in the dark can find it by feel. Dramatics Magazine has a good walkthrough of how a quick change is designed, and the part people underestimate is that the change is choreography, not sewing. Somebody has to be able to do it in a corridor with a flashlight.

That has a knock-on effect on the shape. A garment that opens fully down one side is cut differently from one that pulls over the head, and the seam that carries the closure has to be strong enough to be the only seam holding the piece together during the change.

Why are there duplicates?

Because one costume cannot dry, be repaired and be worn on the same night.

For a tour you build in the numbers a schedule actually needs. Some pieces need two, some need more, and they have to be genuinely identical, which is a discipline of its own. If the second copy fits half a centimetre differently, the performer knows within a minute and you have created a problem in the middle of a show.

Then there are repairs between cities. A hem goes, a hook tears out, a seam under an arm gives. Wardrobe is a workshop that travels. That is what the touring wardrobe part of the collections work is: duplicates where duplicates are needed, and repairs between cities.

What cloth survives a tour?

Cloth that recovers.

Stretch where the movement is, structure where the silhouette is, and lining that will not shred when the piece is pulled off fast. Colour matters more than people expect, because stage light is not neutral and a fabric that reads deep navy in daylight can read black or grey under a wash of colour. Anything that is going near a follow spot gets looked at under light before it gets cut.

I also think about weight and heat honestly. A performer who cannot breathe in a costume will not wear it well, however good it looks in a photograph.

What does that have to do with a dress for a private client?

More than it sounds like, and it comes down to tolerance.

Working on tour teaches you to build for the worst version of the evening rather than the best one. A wedding dress goes through fourteen hours, a car, a meal, dancing and someone standing on the hem. A jacket for a season of performances goes through hundreds of hours. In both cases the question is the same: what happens to this piece when nobody is being careful with it.

So I finish the inside as if it will be seen, put the strength where the load is, and think about how a piece is taken off as well as put on. That is not a stage habit borrowed into couture. It is just the same standard applied to a quieter room.

How does a touring commission actually run?

It runs backwards from a date that will not move.

You arrive with a direction and a deadline. We work out the line-up, what needs duplicating, what has to survive a quick change, and what the schedule does to the numbers. Then patterns, samples, and a run cut and finished properly rather than a large run made quickly.

The first thing I ask is not about cloth. It is when you need it, and what for. Tell me what you are building and when it has to exist, and if you want to see how the atelier works before that, it is described here.

Questions people ask

What makes stage clothes different from ordinary clothes? Quick changes, hot lighting, heavy movement and repeat wear. That shifts the design onto closures, reinforced seams, cloth that recovers, and duplicates built for the schedule.

Do you make duplicates for a tour? Yes, where the schedule needs them. Duplicates have to be genuinely identical, and repairs between cities are part of the same job.

Have you worked on real tours? Yes. Wardrobe tailor on The Monster Ball with Lady Gaga, The Funhouse Carnival Tour with Pink, Last Girl on Earth with Rihanna, and Michael Jackson The Immortal World Tour with Cirque du Soleil. I was also designer and stylist at Eurovision for three consecutive years.

Can you make a single stage piece rather than a whole wardrobe? Yes. A single piece, a line-up for a show, or a wardrobe for a tour are all the same method at different scales.

How long does a touring wardrobe take? It depends on the number of looks and how many duplicates the schedule needs. Bring the date first, because the date decides what is possible.

Made to Measure, Bespoke and Off the Rack: What Is the Actual Difference?

Off the rack is a garment made to a size before you existed. Made to measure adjusts an existing pattern to your measurements. Bespoke draws a new pattern from your body and cuts it once. The words are not legally protected in most markets, so the only reliable question is where the pattern came from.

I have worked on all three sides of this. I have bought for houses, managed shops, and cut for one person at a time. The vocabulary is confusing on purpose in some places and by accident in others, so here is what actually separates them.

What does off the rack mean?

A garment made to a size standard, in a run, before anyone bought it.

Someone drew a pattern for a fit model, that pattern was graded up and down into a size range, and thousands of pieces were cut from it. Then you go into a shop and find the size that is least wrong for you, and a tailor takes in the waist.

There is nothing wrong with this. Most of what most people wear is this, including most of what I wear on a normal Tuesday. What it cannot do is start from you. It starts from an average and then subtracts.

What does made to measure mean?

An existing block pattern, altered to your measurements.

The house has a base pattern that it knows works. Your measurements come in, the pattern is adjusted at a set number of points, and the garment is produced from that. You often choose cloth, lining, buttons and a few construction details. The result fits you considerably better than a rack size and it is built on a shape that was not drawn from your body.

Made to order, which is the phrase the bridal market uses, is usually this. A house has a gown, the gown is produced when you order it, and it is cut in your size with some options attached. That is a genuine service and it is not the same as a garment cut for one person.

What does bespoke mean, and can anyone use the word?

Traditionally, bespoke means a pattern cut from scratch for one client, by hand, with fittings along the way, and the pattern kept afterwards.

Legally, it means less than that. In 2008 the Advertising Standards Authority in the United Kingdom looked at exactly this question after Savile Row tailors objected to a competitor using the word. The ASA found that customers expect a bespoke garment to be made to their measurements and specifications, but do not necessarily expect the pattern to be cut from scratch by hand. Forbes covered the ruling at the time, and the background on bespoke tailoring is worth reading alongside it.

So the word survived and its meaning got wider. Which is why I do not lean on it. I would rather tell you what happens in the room.

What actually happens here?

A conversation, a set of measurements, and then a toile.

I cut a full version of the garment in plain cotton, we fit it, mark it and take it apart, and those corrected cotton pieces become your pattern. Only when the toile is right do I cut the cloth. Then come the fittings, three for a couture piece and more for a wedding dress. The five steps are here.

The pattern is drawn from your body and it stays in the atelier. It is never sold and never used for anyone else. If you come back, we start from a shape that already fits.

I draw it, cut it, fit it and finish it myself. Nothing is made twice.

Which one should you choose?

It depends on what the garment has to do, not on which word sounds most impressive.

Off the rack is right for most of a wardrobe. It is fast, it is available, and for a body close to the size standard it works.

Made to measure is right when you want a good suit or a good gown and the timeline is short. You get a real improvement in fit for a fraction of the process.

A garment cut for one person is right when the fit is the point. A wedding dress. A jacket for a body that no size standard has ever described accurately. A piece for a stage or a camera, where the tolerance is different. Something you intend to still be wearing in ten years. And it is right when you want the thing to be yours in the sense that nobody else has it or will.

How do you tell which one you are being sold?

Ask three questions and listen to how they are answered.

Where does the pattern come from? If the answer is a house block adjusted to you, that is made to measure, and there is no shame in it. If the answer is that a pattern is drawn from your measurements and proved on a toile, that is the other thing.

Is there a toile, and do I fit it? A mock-up in cotton that you personally put on is the clearest signal there is. No toile means an existing pattern.

What happens to the pattern afterwards? Kept for you, or kept for the house, or not kept at all. It tells you who the pattern belongs to.

None of these questions are rude and all three should get a straight answer.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between bespoke and made to measure? Bespoke traditionally means a pattern drawn from scratch for one person and cut by hand, with fittings. Made to measure means an existing house pattern adjusted to your measurements. Legally the words overlap, so ask where the pattern comes from.

Is made to order the same as made to measure? Usually not. Made to order often means a standard design produced after you order it, in a standard size. Made to measure means the pattern is adjusted to your measurements.

Is bespoke a protected term? No. A 2008 ruling by the UK Advertising Standards Authority allowed the word to be used for garments made to a customer's measurements without a hand-cut pattern from scratch.

How can I check what I am actually getting? Ask where the pattern comes from, whether there is a toile you personally fit, and what happens to the pattern afterwards.

Do you make for men as well as women? Yes. Most of the work is for women, but a pattern is drawn from a body and not from a category, so tailoring for men is part of the atelier.
